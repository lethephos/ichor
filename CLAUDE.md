# Ichor — project brief

## What this is

**Ichor** is an interactive family tree of Greek mythology for an English-speaking audience.
Subtitle: *The family tree of Greek myth* (gods and mortals alike, so not “of the Greek gods”).

It shows not only who descends from whom, but marriages, affairs, feuds and killings, transformations and curses. Its main difference from existing family trees is the **versions layer**: every link records which ancient sources tell it, and the user can switch source (Homer, Hesiod, the tragedians, Apollodorus, later writers) and watch the family rearrange itself.

The first branch is **"Two cursed houses"**: the House of Atreus and the House of Labdacus (Thebes), linked through Pelops and Chrysippus. Above them sit the gods (checked against Hesiod, Homer, the Homeric Hymns, Pindar, Euripides, Apollodorus, Pausanias, Hyginus and Ovid): so the tree runs from Gaia and Uranus down to Orestes. Rows: The first gods, Titans and their kin (all twelve of Hesiod, with the Erinyes, and their brothers the Cyclopes and the Hundred-Handers, two groups of three), Zeus and his kin (Zeus and his siblings, the sons of Iapetus, the children of Hyperion, Crius and Coeus, and the goddesses Zeus loved; Nemesis, a daughter of Night, sits there as his lover; Atlas sits there too, with his daughters the Pleiades on a line just below him), The younger gods (the children of Zeus, with Hecate, granddaughter of Coeus; not “Children of Zeus”, since Hecate is not his, and in Hesiod neither are Aphrodite and Hephaestus, and the Moirai are also daughters of Night); then a “Heroes and mortals” divider (not “Mortals”: Harmonia is a goddess, and Semele, Polydeuces, Helen and Iphigenia become immortal in some sources) and rows I–VII. These rows count descent within each house, not time, so they are labelled with bare numerals, not “Generation”: the houses don’t line up (Cadmus to the Epigoni is six steps, his sister Europa to Agamemnon four), so Diomedes sits three rows below Agamemnon, whom he fought beside. A time-aligned layout was considered and rejected: it would have to break either “siblings share a row” or “lower means descended”. The hint says so: “Lower means descended, not later.” Thebes runs Cadmus and Harmonia (I), Polydorus, Agave and Semele (II), Labdacus and Pentheus (III), down to Eteocles and his sisters (VI); Semele’s son Dionysus stays with the gods, so his bloodline is the one drawn upwards. The house of Perseus runs Acrisius and Danaë (I), Perseus, Andromeda and Medusa, with Polydectes of Seriphos and the sea monster (II), the sons of Perseus with Nicippe, a daughter of Pelops, and Amphitryon and Alcmene on a line below (III), down to Heracles, Eurystheus and Iphicles (IV), the row of Agamemnon, with those Heracles fought on his labours: Augeas, Diomedes of Thrace and the centaur Nessus beside him, Hippolyta, queen of the Amazons, on III: Eurystheus is a grandson of Pelops, born on the same day as Heracles. Creon, Jocasta’s brother, links it to Thebes through his daughter Megara. The Argonauts run from Aeolus (I) through Salmoneus, Cretheus, Athamas, Ino and Tyro (II), Phrixus and Helle, Pelias, Aeson and Aeetes (III), to Jason, Medea, Absyrtus, Glauce, Hypsipyle and Orpheus (IV), with Hylas, Heracles’ beloved, and the serpent that guards the fleece; the blind seer Phineus (son of Agenor or of Poseidon, not the brother of Cepheus) sits on Generation I below Agenor, plagued by the Harpies (a group, daughters of the Oceanid Electra, younger gods); Aeetes and Circe are children of Helios, Ino is Cadmus’ daughter, and Heracles, the Dioscuri and Orpheus sail on the Argo. Troy, the house of Dardanus, runs Dardanus, son of Zeus and the Pleiad Electra, with his son Erichthonius on a line below (I), Tros with Ilus, Assaracus and Ganymede below (II), Laomedon and Capys with Priam, Hecuba, Tithonus, Hesione and Anchises below (III), to Hector, Paris, Cassandra, Aeneas and their kin (IV), the row of Agamemnon, and Astyanax (V). Aeacus, Peleus and Thetis lead to Achilles, and his son Neoptolemus marries Hermione, so Troy meets the house of Atreus through Helen, Cassandra and Hermione. The children of Heracles sit on Generation V: Hyllus and Macaria (by Deianira), the sons of Megara (a group: three in Apollodorus and Euripides, eight in Pindar), Telephus, Tlepolemus, and Iolaus, son of Iphicles; Iole and Omphale join Heracles’ row. Hyllus, Iolaus and Alcmene take their revenge on Eurystheus, and Achilles wounds and heals Telephus. Athens runs the earth-born Erichthonius (child of Hephaestus and Gaia, raised by Athena), with his son Pandion below him, and Europa (I); Erechtheus, Procne, Philomela and Tereus, the later Cecrops and Pandion, Minos and Pasiphae (II); Aegeus, Aethra, Itys, Androgeus, Ariadne, Phaedra, the Minotaur, Antiope and Pirithous, and the bandits of the Isthmus road (a group), with Theseus on a line below (III); Hippolytus and Demophon (IV). Theseus links to the Argonauts through Medea, Aegeus’ wife, and to Atreus through Helen, whom he carried off. The Odyssey runs Sisyphus (son of Aeolus), Autolycus (son of Hermes), Arcesius and Nausithous (II); Laertes and Anticleia, Icarius, Alcinous and Arete, and Telamon, son of Aeacus, with Hesione (III); Odysseus, Penelope, Nausicaa, Palamedes, Ajax, Polyphemus (son of Poseidon) and the companions of Odysseus (a group) (IV); Telemachus, Telegonus (his son by Circe) and the suitors of Penelope (a group) (V). Calypso, daughter of Atlas, sits on a line below him. The Sirens (a group, daughters of a Muse) and Scylla (daughter of Phorcys, or of Typhon and Echidna; a nymph Circe turned into a monster in Ovid) sit with the younger gods. Sisyphus is drawn as a second father of Odysseus, as Euripides, Sophocles and Hyginus tell it. A group’s card heads its member list with `count` and `members_label` (“A hundred and eight suitors”; “sisters” by default). The Seven against Thebes adds Calydon, Oeneus and Althaea (III) with Meleager, Deianira’s brother, and Atalanta (IV); Adrastus, Amphiaraus and Eriphyle, Tiresias and Haemon, son of Creon (V); the Seven with Polynices on Generation VI (Tydeus, Capaneus, Parthenopaeus) with Argia, Deipyle and Evadne; and a new row, Generation VII, for the Epigoni: Thersander, Diomedes (who wounds Aphrodite and Ares at Troy) and Alcmaeon. Bellerophon runs from Sisyphus through Glaucus of Corinth (on a line below him) and Iobates of Lycia (II), with Pegasus on a line below Medusa; Bellerophon, Philonoe, Proetus, Stheneboea and the Chimera (III); Hippolochus and Laodamia (IV); Glaucus of Lycia and Sarpedon, son of Zeus (V), who fight for Troy. Patroclus, Achilles’ friend, joins the row of Hector and kills Sarpedon. The house of Minos adds Asterion, Europa’s husband (I); Rhadamanthys, who marries Alcmene, Daedalus and Cocalus (II), with Catreus and Deucalion on a line below Minos; Glaucus and the seer Polyidus, Icarus and Perdix, Talos, and Catreus’ children Clymene and Althaemenes, with Nauplius (III); Idomeneus (IV). Aerope is Catreus’ daughter, so Crete reaches the house of Atreus; Clymene and Nauplius are the parents of Palamedes. Io and the Danaids runs from the river Inachus, son of Ocean (Zeus and his kin), down a chain of lines inside Generation I: Io and Argus; Epaphus; Libya; Belus and Agenor (sons of Poseidon); Danaus, Aegyptus, Cepheus, Phineus, with Cadmus and Europa, Agenor’s children; the Danaids and the sons of Aegyptus (two groups of fifty), Hypermnestra, Lynceus and Amymone; Abas; Acrisius; Danaë. Amymone is the mother of Nauplius, Cepheus and Cassiopeia the parents of Andromeda; Proetus, Acrisius’ twin, stays on the row of Bellerophon. Generation I is tall in the whole tree because of this chain. The monsters adds Tartarus (first gods) and Pontus (Titans and their kin); Nereus, Phorcys, Ceto and Typhon (Zeus and his kin; Typhon drops a line below Hera, who bears him alone in the Homeric Hymn to Apollo); Echidna, the Graeae, the Gorgons, the Nereids (groups) and Ladon (younger gods); Chrysaor below Medusa (II); Orthus and Geryon beside the Chimera (III); the Nemean Lion, the Hydra and Cerberus on the row of Heracles (IV); the Sphinx on the row of Oedipus (V). Parentage of the monsters is left as the sources give it, with Hesiod’s unclear ‘she’ noted on the link. Orpheus and the underworld adds Erebus, Night’s husband (first gods), her sons Hypnos and Thanatos (Titans and their kin), Tityos, son of Gaia (younger gods); Ixion, father of Pirithous (II); Oeagrus, Orpheus’ father, Pheres, son of Cretheus, and the Maenads (a group: Agave, Ino, Autonoe) (III); Eurydice, Admetus and Alcestis, daughter of Pelias (IV). The punishments in Hades (Tantalus, Sisyphus, Ixion, Tityos) are feuds with the god who imposed them. The Trojan War adds Eris, daughter of Night (Titans and their kin row), whose apple brings Hera and Athena’s hatred of Paris; Neleus, Pelias’ twin, Poeas, Oileus, Chryses and Eetion (III); Nestor with Antilochus below him, Memnon, son of Eos and Tithonus, Penthesilea, daughter of Ares, Philoctetes, Ajax the Lesser, Chryseis, Briseis, Helenus and Laocoon (IV), with the killings of Memnon, Penthesilea, Paris, Astyanax and the two Ajaxes’ fates. Prometheus and the fire adds Pandora, Epimetheus’ wife (Zeus and his kin), and Deucalion of the flood (not the Cretan Deucalion), his wife Pyrrha and their son Hellen, father of Aeolus (with the younger gods): so the Titans reach the house of the Argonauts. Rows show a figure’s place in the story rather than a strict generation (Aphrodite, born of Uranus in Hesiod, sits with the younger gods). The Olympians are not a row: they span two, and are marked instead by a small peak beside the medallion (`data/olympians.json`). No poet in our source groups lists all twelve, so the list follows the Parthenon east frieze (Dionysus) and Ennius (Vesta = Hestia), with Plato’s Phaedrus for Hestia; the twelfth seat is hollow. The idea that Hestia gave her seat to Dionysus is modern (Robert Graves, The Greek Myths, 1955, 27.k); it appears only as a “Not in the ancient sources” note on both cards, never as a link or a version. The page heading for the whole tree is "Gods and heroes" (it was "From Gaia to Orestes", which no longer fit once the tree reached Troy, the Argonauts and the Odyssey; "The gods and two cursed houses" sounded wrong before that).

## Current state

The site is live at https://ichor-orpin.vercel.app (Vite + D3, data in `/data`, deployed from `main` by Vercel). The original prototype, `prototype/ichor-atreidae.html`, stays as the reference for behaviour and tone.

- 342 figures, 803 relations, from Gaia and Uranus to Orestes and Antigone, with the house of Perseus down to Heracles, the house of Aeolus down to Jason and Medea, the house of Dardanus down to the fall of Troy, the children of Heracles, Theseus and Athens with the family of Minos, the Odyssey, the Seven against Thebes, Bellerophon, the house of Minos, Io and the Danaids, the monsters, Orpheus and the underworld, and the Trojan War, in rows computed from `generation`; a row grows a line downwards for a child whose parent shares the row (with its spouse), so lower always means descended; no staggering, which made height ambiguous; pan and pinch-zoom.
- **Source switcher**: All versions / Homer & epic / Hesiod / Lyric & tragedy / Apollodorus / Later writers. Links not attested in the chosen source fade out; a one-line note says what changes.
- **Layer toggles**: Bloodlines, Marriages, Affairs, Feuds & killings, Transformations, Curses.
- **Figure card** (who the figure is first, then their links and versions, then the stories, which take the reader away from the card): Greek name, name, tagline, role, story, The Twelve (for Olympians), Changed shape, Made immortal, Family, Feuds and fates (the ring of feuds, curses and transformations, then the lists Feuds & killings / Transformations / Curses with their sources), Where the sources disagree, In the stories (every story the figure takes part in, in any family; each opens at the step where they come in, or at the start if the chosen source skips it; a story the shown family cannot hold says “opens in …” and switches to the smallest family that holds it, so the back button returns; chosen over a search box in the Stories list), Not in the ancient sources (popular ideas with a modern origin, each naming where it comes from: Hestia’s seat, Pandora’s box, Atlas holding the earth, Marlowe’s thousand ships, Hecate as crone, the modern Titan domains), In art (links to the works; a work with a picture shows it as a preview on hover on desktop, or behind a small picture button on phones, loaded only when opened, never embedded in the card itself: the reader chose links with previews over pictures in the card), In the sky (for the 17 figures the ancients set among the stars: Perseus, Andromeda, Cepheus, Cassiopeia, the sea monster (Cetus), Pegasus, Heracles, Ladon (Draco), Ariadne (the Crown), Orpheus (the Lyre), Zeus (the Swan), the Nemean Lion, Castor and Polydeuces, Ganymede (Aquarius), Erichthonius of Athens (the Charioteer) and the Pleiades; who put them there, from Hyginus, Astronomica 2, with Ovid for the Crown and Homer and Hesiod for the Pleiades, and Hevelius’ engraving of the constellation from his Uranographia, 1690, public domain on Commons, shown in the card: the reader asked for the engravings); it sits just before In art, near the end of the card (the reader moved it there).
- **Stories**: the two curses, how Zeus became king, and the myths of Cadmus, Hippodamia, Leda, Iphigenia, Perseus, the labours of Heracles, Theseus and the Minotaur, Jason and the Golden Fleece, the Seven against Thebes, the Trojan War, the wanderings of Odysseus, Prometheus and the fire, the house of Dardanus, Bellerophon and Pegasus, Minos and Daedalus, Io and the Danaids, Down to Hades, the children of Heracles, the loves of Zeus and the brood of Echidna (every family has at least one), told step by step, by all sources or as one source tells it, with “Versions differ” and “Read as” inline.
- **Ariadne’s thread**: the shortest path between two figures, respecting the chosen source.
- **Family**: one section of the card replaces Born to, Married, Affairs, Children and the old Close family map (a hub with the figure in the middle). It is a small genealogical tree, as the chosen source tells it: the parents on top (partners side by side, joined by a double or dashed line; with all versions and more than two parents, each names its sources under it), the figure in gold below them, then each partner (wives and husbands, then lovers, then other parents of the children) hanging from a spine with their children listed under them; children with no other parent named go under “No one else named”. Partners come in bands of three (two for long names); after four bands the rest wait behind “Show N more partners”. The lists with sources sit folded under “Who says so”. Tapping a name moves to them. Chosen over a mind-map layout of the whole tree, which would lose fixed positions when the source changes.
- Light / dark theme switch; search with live filtering.
- **Opening** (`src/styles/intro.css`, `intro()` in `main.js`; the reader asked that the star come first and the page only after the drop): the page starts as the logo’s mark alone in the middle of the screen; the star opens its rays (with a slight turn), then a drop gathers at the south ray and settles into place; once the code and data are in, the mark flies to its place in the header and the rest of the page appears round it (the header’s own mark waits hidden until it lands); while loading goes on, the drop falls away and gathers again. About 1.3 s at the shortest. Then the tree lights up: stars twinkle in from the top of the screen down, their links following (bloodlines, marriages and curses drawn as lines, the rest fading in), about two seconds. With reduced motion the mark stays still and the page simply fades in; if the code never arrives, the mark gives way after 10 s. The opening plays once a session (`sessionStorage` `ichor-opened`, checked in the inline script in the head, which adds `seen`): a reload or a new family link in the same session opens the page at once, with the tree simply fading in.

## Decisions made so far (can be revisited)

**Visual direction**
- Fonts: GFS Didot (display, names, stories, Greek) + Manrope (interface).
- Look: a modern star chart after Hevelius (the default since the reader chose it; styles in `src/styles/stars.css`, over the tokens in `src/styles/tokens.css`). Clean ivory by day, graphite by night (a navy night sky with faint stars was tried; the reader did not like it, and chose graphite over a warm black). Follows the system setting until the reader picks a theme with the sun/moon button, which sits with the whole-tree button (in the top corner of the tree on desktop, in the line of pills on phones; the reader moved it there from the header); the pick is saved in localStorage (`ichor-theme`), and a pick that matches the system is not saved, so the page follows the system again.
  - Gold is ichor, the blood of the gods: transformations, the selected figure, Ariadne’s thread, focus (`--accent`). Red is for curses only.
  - Light: bg `#F5F2EB`, panel `#FCFAF6`, rule `#DDD5C6`, text `#18171B`, lines `#2E2D33`, muted `#5E574C`, curse `#AE2F1A`, gold `#8A5E00`.
  - Dark: bg `#161719`, panel `#1F2023`, rule `#35373C`, text `#ECEAE6`, lines `#B4B2AD`, muted `#A3A19C`, curse `#E8604A`, gold `#E8B54A`.
  - The earlier palette “pale clay and gold ichor” (light bg `#E8CFB2`, dark `#14100C`, medallion nodes) stays in `tokens.css` and shows with `?look=clay`; the antique atlas with `?look=atlas` (below).
- Logo (reworked with the star-chart look, after many rounds; the earlier iota wordmark, “ichor” with a gold breathing, was dropped: the reader wanted a fresh start tied to what ichor is): the mark is a four-rayed star, like the stars on the tree, whose south ray runs into a drop of gold: the light of the gods turns into their blood, and the stars recall the heroes they set in the sky. Its shape, settled step by step with the reader: side rays shorter than the upright ones (7 to 11), a small heart where the rays meet (fine cubic curves hugging the axes, not the fuller sparkle), a narrow drop whose fine, curved-in tip meets the south ray. Gold only, never red. The wordmark is the mark beside “Ichor” in GFS Didot, capital I (outlined from the font; a lower-case i was tried and the reader kept the capital), with the subtitle under it in the header (in the header itself the lockup differs, below). The mark is a little taller than the capitals (1.14 times) and aligned to their top: its north ray is level with the top of the I and the drop dips just below the line, as round letters do (chosen over the star centred on the letters, the whole mark centred, and a mark exactly as tall as the capitals). The favicon and app icons use the mark alone, drawn a little fuller (wider heart and drop) so it holds at 16px. The geometry is generated by a script (star and drop helpers, glyphs from GFS Didot via fontTools); in the header the lockup is “Ichor”, then the mark standing between the word and the subtitle, which runs beside it in two lines (“The family tree / of Greek myth”; the reader chose the mark over a hairline divider there, and the subtitle beside the word over under it); on phones the subtitle shows too (hidden below 360px wide), and the mark stands on the divider between the Family and Source cells below it (those two cells share the line evenly, so the divider does not move with the family’s name; the gap before the mark is computed in CSS from the screen width, so it is right from the first frame); the word and the mark are inline SVG in the header kicker (`.wordmark`, `.wm-sep`), coloured by `--text` and `--accent` (`.wm-ink`, `.wm-mark`); the files in `brand/` and `og.png` keep the mark before the word; sources in `brand/`, icons, manifest and the link card `og.png` in `public/`. Icons and the link card follow the star-chart palette (ivory `#F5F2EB`, ink `#18171B`, graphite `#161719` for the dark favicon). In running text the name stays “Ichor”.
- Small medallion nodes: gods have a double ring, figures from other cycles a dashed ring, a diamond badge marks a transformation, a small gold star marks a mortal made immortal (Semele, Cadmus, Helen, Menelaus, the Dioscuri, Iphigenia, Heracles, Ino, Ganymede, Tithonus, Ariadne, Amphiaraus, Pegasus, Io, Memnon; it hides when the chosen source doesn’t tell it), a small peak marks one of the Twelve Olympians (hollow for Hestia and Dionysus); a group (the Muses, the Fates…) is a ringed medallion holding three small dots.
- Row names sit in a column pinned to the left edge of the screen on desktop (small caps, interface font, fixed size at any zoom); the tree slides beneath it and the names follow their rows up and down. The “Heroes and mortals” divider is named there too. On phones there is no column (it took too much width) and no framed tags: each name is written on its row’s top rule, in small caps, at the start of the line, and moves with the tree, so it shows only when the left end of the tree is in view (the reader chose this over names that wait at the screen edge). The divider’s name sits just above its line. When the tree is zoomed far out (the whole tree) the rows are too close for names, so they hide.
- Star-atlas look (a mockup, only with `?look=atlas`, styles in `src/styles/atlas.css`; `&frame=none` hides the border): after Hevelius’ Uranographia (1690). Light = old paper (grain, foxing, a darker edge; laid and chain lines were tried and dropped, the reader did not like the stripes), dark = the night sky. Figures are stars as Hevelius drew them: a bright one (gods, and figures with many links) a dark seal with a jagged rim and a pale star inside, a faint one a small six-rayed star; a group three small stars. Names in IM Fell English (17th-century type): mortals in italic, gods and groups in small capitals. The border is Hevelius’ chequered band between two rules. The logo stays as it is. Under discussion with the reader: she was unsure of the border and asked for other stars and more paper (second round). The modern variant, now the site’s default look, keeps Hevelius’ idea without the antique dress (`src/styles/stars.css`): clean ivory by day, graphite by night, no frame and no paper; a bright figure is a four-pointed sparkle in a fine ring, a faint one the sparkle alone; mortals in GFS Didot, gods, groups and section heads in spaced Manrope capitals; hairline links (a marriage is two hairlines, a wider line with a band of the background down its middle: a single hairline for every link had hidden marriages behind that band; the links of a selected figure, a story step, the thread or a Guide example come forward slightly thicker). The rows stay (the reader asked whether a star chart needs them; they carry “lower means descended” and fixed places across sources), drawn as a chart’s graticule: dotted parallels with graduations, faint dotted meridians every 240px, the gods–mortals divider ruled and graduated like the ecliptic, and row numerals on desktop with a short tick like a margin scale. Buttons are square, like the cells of a table (the reader asked: rounded pills did not fit the star chart); buttons that belong together share their rules: the source pills, the theme switch and the whole-tree button, the card navigator, the phone tools bar and the phone line of pills. On phones those two lines are rows of a table rather than boxes: the line of pills is ruled above and below, with hairline dividers between its cells running the full height of the row, and the tools are a strip along the bottom edge, ruled on top, with dividers (the reader suggested dividers inside the line instead of boxes inside a box). The Guide button is a cell of the header too, as wide as the theme and whole-tree cells below it, its divider running into theirs (the reader asked). The header icons are hairlines in the same spirit: the moon a sparkle beside it, the sun is the astronomer’s ☉ (a ring with a dot) with eight short rays, and the whole-tree button is four corners round a sparkle. The engraving in In the sky is shown as a line drawing (ink multiplied into the ivory, or inverted to light lines on the graphite); a tap shows the plate as printed.
- Edge styles: descent = thin solid; marriage = double line; affair = dashed; feud = dash-dot; transformation = dotted gold with arrow; curse = red with arrow.

- Card bar: the card has a bar on top that stays in view as it scrolls, with a close button (×, replacing the “Close” text that scrolled away) and the figure’s or panel’s name, which appears once the big name scrolls off. On phones the bar is also the grip: pull up for full height, down to shrink or close; the sheet opens at about 62% of the screen. The header also folds once the reader moves the tree (pan or zoom, not a tap), and comes back with “Whole tree” or a new family. On phones the header is one line: the wordmark and a Guide button (a word; it was an “i” icon, then “About”; the reader chose “Guide”, since the panel is mostly how to read the tree); the hint (“Lower means descended, not later”), the line styles and the source groups live in the Guide panel (“Guide to Ichor”) it opens in the card sheet. While a card is open on a phone, the one-line header folds away and only the line of pills stays, led by the logo’s mark (the star and its drop, in gold; the reader asked to keep it in view), so the tree gets the room above the card; when the card closes, the header comes back and the view stays centred on what was shown (the reader found the tree cramped and closing a long card meant scrolling back up). Desktop has the same Guide button in the top right corner of the header, opening the Guide in the card. The Guide is a live legend (the reader asked for it to be interactive): each line style can be tapped to show one example on the tree (Atreus and Agamemnon, Zeus and Hera, Zeus and Leda, Atreus and Thyestes, Zeus and Io, Myrtilus and Pelops, or the best-attested link of that kind in the family shown), with its sentence and sources; “Lower means descended, not later” shows Agamemnon and Diomedes, three rows apart though they fought side by side (when both don’t fit at a readable size, the camera shows Atreus and Agamemnon, then glides to Tydeus and Diomedes); each source can be tapped to switch the tree to it, with its note under it, and tapped again for all versions. An example a family does not hold says so. “Take the tour →” at the top of the Guide (the reader asked for it) opens “A minute with Ichor”, five steps on the whole tree (it switches there from a family), each lit up and framed like a step of a story, with Back, dots and Next: the stars (Zeus and Hera); the lines (the house of Atreus: Pelops, Aerope, Thyestes, Agamemnon, with the curse); lower means descended (Agamemnon and Diomedes, with the glide); the sources (Agamemnon’s fathers, with All versions / Homer & epic / Hesiod to switch); Stories and the thread (Oedipus to Orestes through Pelops’ curse on Laius), ending with Open Stories, Try the thread and Done. The theme switch stays left of the whole-tree button, which keeps the corner as the more used of the two. The rows were renamed from “Titans” and “Children of the Titans” to “Titans and their kin” and “Zeus and his kin”, since those rows also hold the Cyclopes, the Hundred-Handers, the Erinyes, Pontus, Hypnos, Thanatos and Eris (Hesiod’s Titans are only the twelve, Theogony 133–138), and Nemesis, Typhon and Nereus, who are not children of Titans.
- Tools: on desktop the layer toggles, Trace a curse, Ariadne’s thread and the search box float in a dock at the bottom of the tree; on phones four buttons (Layers, Curses, Thread, Find) float at the bottom and open panels in the card sheet. "Whole tree" sits in the top corner of the tree, with the theme switch beside it; while a card is open they move to the left of the card.

**Tone**
- Taglines read like a family drama ("Served his brother a dinner of his own children."). Short sentences. Stories told step by step are in the present tense; the story on a figure’s card is in the past tense (present only for what is still true).
- All user-facing text in English, with typographic quotes and apostrophes.

**Sources**
- Groups: E = Homer, Homeric Hymns, Cypria; H = Hesiod (Theogony, Catalogue of Women); T = Pindar, Stesichorus, Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides; A = Apollodorus, Library; L = Pausanias, Hyginus, Ovid.
- Every relation must list its sources. Never add a link or a version without a source.

## Target structure

Keep it simple: a static site, no backend.

- Vite + vanilla TypeScript (or plain JS) + D3.
- Data out of the code, in `/data`:
  - `people.json` — id, name, greek, gender, generation, kind (god / nymph / mortal / other-cycle / group), branch, role, tagline, story, versions, modern (notes on what the ancient sources do not say, each naming its modern origin), transformation, immortal (text and sources), art, sky (constellation, text, cite, sources, image); a group adds count, members and members_note.
  - `relations.json` — type, from, to, sources, note.
  - `stories.json` — stories told step by step: the two curses and further myths (`kind`: curse or story). A story marked `draft` still needs checking against the sources; the two curses, how Zeus became king and the myths of Cadmus, Hippodamia, Leda, Iphigenia, Perseus, Heracles, Theseus, Jason, the Seven against Thebes, the Trojan War, the Odyssey, Prometheus, Dardanus, Bellerophon, Minos, Io, Hades, the children of Heracles, the loves of Zeus and the brood of Echidna have been checked. A step is told by a source when the links it reaches first (from its figures to people already met) are attested there; a step can list its own `pairs` when its figures reach further than it tells (Medea meets Jason in Colchis, not yet Pelias), and those links wait for their step on the tree. The camera frames the step’s figures and the other ends of the links that tell it.
  - `sources.json` — source groups and their labels.
- Styles as design tokens (CSS variables) in one file; components separate (tree, card, controls, curse panel, thread panel).
- Layout computed from data (generation tiers), so new branches can be added by editing JSON only.
- Deploy to Vercel.
